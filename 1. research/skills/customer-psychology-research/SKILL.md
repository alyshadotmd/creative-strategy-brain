# Customer Psychology Research Framework

## In the brain

| Reads | Where |
|---|---|
| What customers actually said | `1. research/customer/reviews/` and the review audit |
| Organic conversation | pulled using `1. research/customer/keyword-bank.md` |
| What the product is | `brand/products/` |

**Writes to** `1. research/customer/personas/` — one file per persona, each carrying its trigger moments, in the shape `creative-engine` and `persona-messaging-matrix` read.

This runs after reviews are in and before the engine. It deepens personas rather than replacing them: `review-audit` establishes what customers say, this establishes the state they are in when they say it.

This skill defines the research process for understanding your customer at a psychological and situational level — **before** you start building creative strategy.

**This skill is UPSTREAM of the Creative Strategy Engine.** It produces the insight that makes messaging angles real. Without this layer, you're writing ads based on demographics. With it, you're writing ads based on lived human experience.

## How This Fits in the Stack

```
Customer Psychology Research (this skill)
    ↓
Produces: Generational context + Micro-moments + Emotional mapping
    ↓
Creative Strategy Engine
    ↓
Defines messaging angles built on real psychological insight
    ↓
Hook Writing / Visual Formats / Creative Mechanics
    ↓
Ad execution that actually resonates
```

## What We're Actually Researching

Creative strategy used to be about formats, messages, and offers. This framework is about something harder and more important: **how to communicate to your customer.** Not what to say — how to say it, when, and why it will land given where they are in their life.

To do this, we research three things in order:

1. **Generational Insights** — the world that shaped them before your product existed
2. **Micro-Moments** — the specific situations where their pain surfaces the hardest
3. **Emotional Mapping** — what they're actually feeling in those moments

---

## Part 1: Generational Insights

**The world that shaped your customer before your product existed.**

Generational research is not about demographics. It's about understanding the informational environment, economic experiences, and cultural forces that formed how this person processes the world — because those forces shape what formats feel familiar, what claims feel credible, and what makes them open or closed to spending money.

### What to Research

**1. Their childhood informational environment**

What was the primary medium they grew up consuming? This tells you which presentation formats their brain already trusts.

- Grew up reading long-form magazine articles → editorial/press formats feel credible and familiar
- Grew up watching infomercials → demonstration formats with proof and urgency feel normal, not weird
- Grew up on YouTube → talking head, tutorial-style formats feel authentic
- Grew up on TikTok → fast cuts, direct address, native-feeling UGC is the default expectation

Almost any format can be borrowed from what your customer grew up consuming — nutrition labels, medical scans, receipts, report cards, newspaper front pages. If their brain processed that format as a child, it processes it faster as an adult.

**2. Their relationship with money**

What shaped how this customer thinks about spending? This tells you whether price anchoring, value framing, or luxury positioning will work — and whether financial objections are psychological or practical.

- Came of age during the 90s economic boom → "treat yourself" framing works, spending feels normal
- Graduated during the 2008 financial crisis → "investment" framing works better than "splurge," ROI language lands
- Parents said "we can't afford it" regularly → scarcity anchoring is baked in, discounts feel like relief not manipulation
- Witnessed institutional financial collapse → distrust of corporate price claims, needs peer validation over brand authority

**3. What their life looks like right now**

Their generational context tells you HOW they think. Their current life stage tells you WHERE they are and what feels urgent. We want to slot into their life where we actually fit.

- Building a career → time-poor, status-sensitive, efficiency is the love language
- Aging kids and teenagers → identity shifting, looking back at who they used to be, redefining themselves
- Exhausted with a lack of structure → fatigue-driven decisions, anything that simplifies is appealing
- Empty nester → sudden freedom, new spending patterns, rediscovering personal identity

Ask: what tension makes an ad feel relevant to their life *today*, exactly in the moment that they see it?

**4. How they evaluate risk and trust authority**

This determines whether you lead with proof, lead with peer voices, lead with credentialed experts, or lead with raw unfiltered testimonials.

- Gen Z / younger Millennials who watched institutions fail → distrustful of official authority, responds to subreddits, real customer reviews, raw unfiltered UGC. "Studies show" falls flat. "Reddit agrees" lands.
- Older Millennials / Gen X who grew up with doctor-as-authority → doctor endorsement, clinical studies, and expert credentials carry weight
- Baby Boomers who trust established institutions → brand longevity and institutional legitimacy matter, newer-is-not-better
- High skeptics across any generation → 1-star review mining, objection pre-emption, "I was skeptical too" testimonials

### Where to Research Generational Insights

| When You Need | Go Here | Look For |
|---|---|---|
| Generational context and worldview | Pew Research Center, academic generational studies | Formative economic events, technology adoption timelines, trust patterns |
| How your audience talks about their life | Reddit (age-specific subs), Facebook Groups, Quora | Language patterns, complaints, what they celebrate vs. what frustrates them |
| Cultural and economic forces shaping decisions | Bureau of Labor Statistics, Census data, news archives | Cost of living shifts, career patterns, family structure trends |
| Real conversation patterns by age group | YouTube comment sections, podcast reviews, Amazon Q&A | Tone, vocabulary, level of skepticism, what questions they ask |

**Why this matters for format selection:** Generational worldview tells you *which proof formats land*, *which authority signals work*, and *which vocabulary sounds like a friend vs. a marketer*.

## Part 2: Micro-Moments

**The tiny situations where the problem surfaces the most and is most salient.**

Understanding your customer's generational view doesn't tell you about the moments that matter. A good strategist finds pain points. A great strategist finds out *when* those pain points show up and *why* — the exact moment in the year, the week, and the day that the problem gets triggered.

**The difference:**

❌ **Generic pain:** She wants to lose weight.

✅ **Micro-moment:** She's getting dressed with a friend who just lost 40 lbs. Her jeans don't fit. She's getting ready to go out and she's anxious and self-conscious in a way that catches her off guard.

Hyper-specific situations like the second one give you something to *write towards*. They tell you who to hire, what to say, what the setting looks like, and what emotion is present. The first one gives you a brief that anyone could have written.

### The Micro-Moment Has Four Properties

1. **Where it happens** — physical setting, social context, alone vs. in front of others
2. **When it happens** — time of day, time of year, life stage trigger, specific recurring situation
3. **What makes it acute** — why is the problem louder *right now* vs. yesterday?
4. **What they do or don't do** — how does the problem affect their behavior or choices in this moment?

### Examples by Category

**Wedding ring brand:**
- Generic: Men who lose their wedding rings
- Micro-moment: He takes off his ring before the gym again. It's been sitting in his gym bag for two weeks. He tells himself he'll put it back on but he doesn't. His wife noticed but hasn't said anything yet.

**Weight management:**
- Generic: Women who want to lose weight
- Micro-moment: She's scrolling Instagram while her husband watches TV. She stops on a photo of herself from three years ago. She doesn't look sad — she looks tired. It's 10:47pm.

**Focus supplement:**
- Generic: Remote workers who have trouble focusing
- Micro-moment: It's 2pm on a Tuesday. He's been on his laptop for four hours and he can't tell if he's worked or just switched tabs. His Slack has 14 unread messages. He closes the laptop.

The more specific, the better. Specificity signals to the audience: *this ad was made for me.*

### Where to Research Micro-Moments

| When You Need | Go Here | Look For |
|---|---|---|
| Real trigger moments and daily friction | Reddit threads, Facebook Groups, niche forums | Specific situations described in first person ("I was at the dentist and...") |
| Emotional language around the problem | Amazon reviews (1-star and 5-star), Trustpilot | Before/after descriptions, what finally made them buy, what they wish they'd known |
| Unfiltered customer voice | Qualitative surveys, quiz data, CS tickets | Why they bought NOW (not why they bought in general) |
| Social comparison moments | TikTok/Instagram comments on related content | What people tag friends in, what gets "this is SO me" responses |

**The "why now" signal is your hook.** Whatever tipped someone from thinking about buying to actually buying — that's the micro-moment. That's what your hook should name.

## Part 3: Emotional Mapping

**Which emotion is the person actually feeling in this specific micro-moment?**

For each micro-moment you identify, map the emotional state that's present. This tells you *how* to communicate, not just *what* to communicate.

The same message delivered in the wrong emotional register fails. If your customer is privately ashamed and you message "join the community," that falls flat. If they've been suffering alone and you say "you don't have to do this alone," that lands.

### The Valence-Intensity Model

Use this two-axis framework to map emotion precisely:

**Axis 1 — Valence (Positive vs. Negative)**
Is the emotion pleasant or unpleasant to experience?

**Axis 2 — Intensity (Low vs. High)**
How strong is the feeling? Is it a low hum or an acute spike?

```
                    POSITIVE
                        |
         Contentment    |    Excited / Elated
         Satisfied      |    Inspired
                        |
LOW ——————————————————————————————————— HIGH
INTENSITY                               INTENSITY
                        |
         Irritated      |    Rage / Fury
         Anxious        |    Shame / Despair
         Bored          |    Fear / Panic
                        |
                    NEGATIVE
```

### Why This Matters for Creative

**High intensity negative emotions** (shame, fear, panic, despair):
- These are usually private. People don't want to see themselves reflected publicly in these states.
- Lead with empathy and recognition. Don't dramatize or amplify — you'll make them feel worse, not seen.
- "You don't have to do this alone" works. "We know exactly how bad this feels" might backfire.
- Avoid urgency or scarcity tactics here — they layer anxiety onto anxiety.

**Low intensity negative emotions** (irritation, mild frustration, boredom):
- More public, more shareable. "This is so me" energy lives here.
- Pattern interrupt and humor work. This is the micro-moment content that gets tagged and shared.
- Relatability is the primary hook mechanism.

**High intensity positive emotions** (excitement, aspiration, pride):
- Buy into the feeling. The product is the ticket to more of this.
- Show the transformation at its most elevated. Aspirational UGC, before/after narratives.
- Social proof and community work here — "people like you are already feeling this."

**Low intensity positive emotions** (contentment, satisfaction, relief):
- This is often the *after* state your customer wants. Show them landing here.
- Quiet testimonials, calm demonstrations, "life without the problem" format.
- Don't over-hype — they don't want excitement, they want peace.

### Three Additional Emotional Context Questions

1. **Is this emotion experienced alone or in front of others?**
   - Alone: Privacy-first messaging. "Just you and this problem, we get it."
   - In front of others: Social stakes messaging. "How you show up matters."

2. **Is this emotion acute (sudden onset) or chronic (long-simmering)?**
   - Acute: Urgency is appropriate. "When it hits, you need a solution that works fast."
   - Chronic: Patience and empathy. "You've been dealing with this for a long time."

3. **Does the customer have hope or have they given up?**
   - Has hope: Product-as-solution framing works. Transformation messaging.
   - Has given up: Lead with "I was skeptical too." Re-open possibility before selling.

## Part B: How Do We Say It? (TEEP Format Selection)

Once you understand *who* you're talking to (generational context + micro-moments + emotional state), you need to match your creative format to the **psychological state of the person processing the ad.**

We are not competing with other ads. We are competing with a brain that does not actively want to click and go somewhere else. Format selection is about matching your offer to the cognitive state of the person receiving it.

A brain in exploration mode rejects anything pushy. A brain in decision mode rejects anything that over-elaborates and prevents action. Format mismatch = scroll.

### The TEEP Framework

People move through four phases. Your creative format must match the phase.

```
TRIGGER → EXPLORATION → EVALUATION → PURCHASE
```

Note: People cycle fluidly through Exploration and Evaluation. They are not a clean funnel — they're a psychological state. Your job is to match the state, not force a sequence.

---

### Trigger

**State:** Pre-awareness. Life is just happening. They don't know they're in-market yet.

**Your job:** Name a moment they're already living, so they feel *seen* — not sold to. You are not selling anything here. You are making someone feel recognized.

**Format match:**
- Micro-moment scene recreations
- Relatable situational hooks ("When you're getting dressed and...")
- "This is so me" content — low friction, high recognition
- Pattern interrupts that name their world

**Why this format:** They don't know they're in-market yet. Heavy sales content will be skipped or resented. You earn attention by proving you understand their life, not by promoting your product.

**Ad example (Ridge):** *"3 out of 4 men misplace their wedding band in their lifetime"* — a statistic that names a moment, not a product pitch.

---

### Exploration

**State:** Low commitment, high novelty. They've recognized the problem exists and they're casually looking. But they are not ready to invest attention.

**Your job:** Earn the right to say more. This is not the place for specs and benefits. Heavy content here feels like a sales pitch and gets skipped.

**Format match:**
- Short-form video with strong visual hooks
- Memes, pattern interrupts, unexpected angles
- Story-driven content that opens curiosity without closing a sale
- Visual hooks that stop the scroll and promise something interesting

**Why this format:** They're browsing, not buying. You need to earn their attention before you ask for their consideration. A brain in this state has a short patience window.

**Ad example (Ridge):** *"He finally wears his ring after 7 years of marriage"* — story-first, product-secondary. Opens curiosity, doesn't close a sale.

---

### Evaluation

**State:** Medium commitment, actively comparing options. They're gathering evidence. They need to know this works for people like them.

**Your job:** Build credibility. This is where proof is most powerful — and where the wrong kind of proof (wrong messenger, wrong format) destroys trust instead of building it.

**Format match:**
- Testimonials from people who match the viewer's persona
- Before/after formats with specificity
- Educational content that explains WHY this works
- Head-to-head comparisons vs. alternatives
- Case studies with real details (not vague claims)

**Why this format:** They're actively comparing you against alternatives. They need evidence — not promises. Social proof from people who look like them is more persuasive than any brand claim you can make.

**Ad example (Ridge):** *Ridge vs. Others comparison chart* — lifetime replacements, scratch resistance, premium materials. Direct comparison, proof-based, made for someone mid-evaluation.

---

### Purchase

**State:** High commitment, ready to buy. They need friction removed. Unanswered questions at this stage kill conversions.

**Your job:** Close the loop. The barrier to buying is not awareness — it's residual doubt, missing information, or one unanswered objection. Address that objection.

**Format match:**
- Objection handling (explicit "But what about...?" resolution)
- Guarantees, risk reversal, returns policy front and center
- Urgency-based creative (sales, deadlines, limited inventory) — only works here
- Founder stories that build final trust
- Product demonstrations that answer the last question
- Long-form content that gives them permission to act

**Why this format:** They're ready to buy but need friction removed. Unanswered questions at this stage kill conversions. This is the one place where urgency and scarcity are appropriate — because the hesitation is real, not manufactured.

**Ad example (Ridge):** *"These deals won't last — Anniversary Sale, up to 32% off"* — urgency only works here because the customer is already warm.

---

### TEEP Format Selection Reference

| Phase | State | Right Format | Wrong Format |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Trigger** | Pre-aware, life is happening | Micro-moment scenes, "this is so me" content, relatable hooks | Product pitches, benefit lists, CTAs |
| **Exploration** | Low commitment, high novelty | Short-form video, visual hooks, memes, pattern interrupts | Heavy specs, long testimonials, comparison charts |
| **Evaluation** | Medium commitment, needs proof | Testimonials, before/afters, educational content, comparisons | Urgency, scarcity, hard sell |
| **Purchase** | High commitment, ready to act | Objection handling, guarantees, urgency, demos | Content that re-opens doubt or overcomplicates the decision |

## How to Use This Framework

### Research Sequence

Run these three research tracks in order before building any creative strategy:

```
Step 1: Generational Research
→ What shaped this customer's worldview, money relationship, trust patterns?
→ Output: Format preferences, authority signals that work, vocabulary that sounds native

Step 2: Micro-Moment Mapping
→ What are the 3-5 most specific situations where this pain spikes?
→ Output: Real scenes to write towards, hooks that name their world, casting direction

Step 3: Emotional Mapping
→ For each micro-moment: what emotion is present, how intense, private or public?
→ Output: Tone and register for each piece of creative, messaging do's and don'ts

Step 4: TEEP Mapping
→ For each micro-moment: which phase of the TEEP framework is this customer in?
→ Output: Format selection for each piece of creative
```

### What This Feeds Into

The output of this framework directly informs:

- **Messaging angles** in the Creative Strategy Engine (now grounded in real emotional insight, not demographics)
- **Hook writing** (hooks can now name a specific micro-moment and emotional state, not just a pain category)
- **Format selection** (TEEP tells you which visual format fits before you open the format library)
- **Casting and creative direction** (micro-moment specificity tells you who to hire and what the scene looks like)

### Deliverable Format

For each persona being researched, produce a **Customer Psychology Brief** with:

1. **Generational Context Summary** (3-5 key worldview points: money relationship, authority bias, format familiarity, current life stage tension)
2. **Top 3 Micro-Moments** (hyper-specific situational descriptions, not pain categories)
3. **Emotional Map per Micro-Moment** (valence, intensity, public/private, acute/chronic, hope/hopeless)
4. **TEEP Profile** (which phases are most underserved by current creative? where is the brand missing?)

## What This Skill Is Not

This is a **research and insight skill**, not an execution skill.

It does NOT:
- Write hooks (use Hook Writing skill)
- Select specific visual formats (use Visual Formats skill after TEEP mapping)
- Build messaging angles (use Creative Strategy Engine)
- Write scripts or briefs (use downstream execution skills)

It DOES:
- Tell you who you're actually talking to
- Tell you when the problem is most real for them
- Tell you what emotional register to operate in
- Tell you which phase of TEEP your creative should match
- Give you something real to write towards

## REMEMBER

### The Core Shift

Creative strategy used to be about picking formats, writing messages, and choosing offers. This framework is about understanding the psychological and situational reality of your customer well enough that your creative slots into their life naturally — not as an interruption, but as a recognition.

The best ads don't feel like ads. They feel like someone finally said the thing the customer has been feeling but never heard out loud.

This framework is how you figure out what that thing is.

### The Test for a Good Micro-Moment

Ask yourself: could I cast this scene? If yes, it's specific enough. If your micro-moment is still an abstraction ("when they're feeling insecure about their weight"), it's not a micro-moment — it's a pain category. Keep going until you can describe what they're wearing, who they're with, and what they do next.

### The Test for Emotional Mapping

Ask yourself: if I showed my customer this ad, would the emotional register feel *right* or *off*? A person who is privately ashamed watching a high-energy "join the community" ad will feel misunderstood, not inspired. Match the register to the emotion, not the emotion you want them to have by the end.

### The Test for TEEP Match

Ask yourself: what does this brain need right now? Not what does the brand need — what does the brain in this state need to feel comfortable taking the next step? Give them that. One step at a time.
