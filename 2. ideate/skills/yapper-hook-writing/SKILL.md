# Yapper Hook Writing

## In the brain

| Reads | Where |
|---|---|
| Voice of customer — the words the hook is built from | `1. research/customer/reviews/` and the review audit |
| Post-purchase surveys, call transcripts | `1. research/customer/` |
| Persona, awareness stage, trigger moment | `1. research/customer/personas/` |
| The angle and the product truth under it | `2. ideate/messaging-angles/`, `brand/products/` |
| What may and may not be said | `brand/claims.md` |
| How this brand and platform sound | `3. create/voice/` |

If none of the voice-of-customer sources exist yet, stop and say so rather than writing from the brand's own marketing — that is the one source guaranteed to contain nobody's language but the brand's.

**Writes to** the concept's `script.md` in `pipeline/`, where the chosen system becomes the opening beat. Systems worth keeping go into `2. ideate/hooks/openers-spoken.md`, tagged with stage, persona and trigger moment.

**Scope.** This skill owns hooks for the yapper format specifically — a person speaking to camera. It does not cover statics or written-only overlays; those need a hook skill written for a surface where nobody talks.

**Claims are blocking.** A hook that cannot clear `brand/claims.md` is not a hook, whatever it does in review. Check before writing, not after.


This skill writes **hook systems**, not hook lines.

A hook is the first second of an ad working across three channels at once:

| Channel | What it is | Its job |
|---|---|---|
| **Text overlay hook** | The first line of on-screen text the viewer reads | Context + curiosity, in a fraction of a second |
| **Verbal hook** | The first sentence said out loud | The human moment — sounds like a real person talking |
| **Visual hook** | What's happening in frame | Establishes the person, the place, and the moment |

The text overlay and the verbal are the primary deliverables. The visual is a suggestion that pairs with them. The three say **different** things — but together they instantly convey the core idea of the ad and make the viewer need to see the rest.

---

## Rule zero: the hook is a filter, not a net

A hook's job is not attention. Attention is cheap — anything loud gets attention.

The job is to make the **right person** want to keep watching. One very specific person, in a specific moment, feeling a specific way. Everyone else can scroll. A hook the wrong person scrolls past is working.

Before writing anything, be able to answer both of these:

- **Who stops?** One sentence: the person + the moment they're in + how they feel in it.
- **Who scrolls?** If you can't name who this *isn't* for, it isn't targeted. "Anyone might like this" means no one will feel seen.

---

## Rule one: no example hooks live in this skill — on purpose

Hooks written from examples sound like the examples. Every literal hook line that sits in a skill leaks into the output, no matter which brand or persona is on the table. So this skill explains **what** each piece must do, never **how** a finished one reads.

What this means when writing:

- Do not recycle openers, phrasings, scenarios, or personas from hooks you've seen before — in this skill, in your training, in past outputs. Every line gets built from this brand's voice of customer.
- If a draft feels familiar, that's the tell. Throw it out and go back to the source material.
- The only hooks worth imitating are the ones the customers already half-wrote in their reviews and comments.

The one exception: reference hooks the user pastes in as source material for this specific job.

## Channel 1: The text overlay hook

It gets read in a fraction of a second. So:

- **10 words maximum. Aim for 3–8.**
- It looks like text the person in the video would slap over their own TikTok to add a little context — typed in five seconds, casual, plain words, the way the persona actually texts. Not a headline. Not a title. Not a lesson.
- It leaves something hanging — something unsaid, an unanswered question, an unexplained claim. That open loop is what makes the viewer actually listen to the verbal.

The overlay does one of three jobs. Pick one:

1. **Name the unnamed.** Supply the noun or context the verbal deliberately leaves vague — what's being tried, tested, or talked about — so the spoken line never has to say it.
2. **Call out the viewer.** A short question or address aimed at the exact person, worded the way they'd say it themselves, that makes the right viewer go "wait — me?"
3. **Make a claim the video has to cash.** A first-person statement of identity or feeling — ideally a lifted customer phrase — that the verbal reacts to and the visual proves.

Register test: a headline explains to an audience; a context line talks like the persona. Title case, numbered-guide framing, how-to energy, or anything that could be a YouTube title or blog header = rewrite it.

---

## Channel 2: The verbal hook

The first sentence out of their mouth. One non-negotiable standard: **it must sound like the actual start of something a real person would say.** Mid-thought. On their phone. Like the thought just occurred to them.

- **Cadence comes from the voice of customer — not from a stock list of openers.** Before writing a single verbal, study how these customers actually construct sentences in the reviews, comments, or transcripts: how they open a thought, their filler words, their slang, their generation's rhythm. The verbal should sound like one of *them* talking, not like a generic creator voice.
- The verbal carries the emotion of the system: vulnerability, relatability, a confession, a first impression, a realization.
- **Vague referents are a feature.** Real speech points before it names — "this," "these," "it" — and lets context do the naming. In a hook, the overlay is that context. The mouth never has to say the product's name or category.
- Contractions always. 6th–8th grade words. Fragments are fine. Read it out loud — if you stumble, or it sounds like an email, rewrite it.

Never in a verbal hook: the brand name, a product introduction, a question that diagnoses the viewer's problem at them, benefit-speak, or a sentence that fully explains the product. If the verbal makes complete sense with the overlay hidden, it's probably an ad read.

---

## Channel 3: The visual hook (suggested)

Suggest one visual per system — this skill focuses on the overlay + verbal pair, but every system should come with the visual that belongs to it.

The visual's job is to establish **who** we're talking to and **the moment** — so precisely that the conversation the overlay and verbal are about to have makes sense to start right here. It is not a storyboard of the ad. Define it with four elements:

1. **Framing.** Where is the camera, and why would it be there? The placement should be explainable in one phrase — propped on a counter, held selfie-style, set on a shelf. If you can't say where the phone is sitting, the shot isn't native.
2. **Location.** The exact place where this thought would actually occur. If she'd never have this thought here, move her.
3. **Person.** She should read as the persona at a glance — her state, her context, what kind of day she's having.
4. **Action.** One simple thing she's doing. One.

Hard rules:

- **One shot, one action, one take.** If describing it needs a cut, a sequence, or more than one "then," it's a storyboard, not a hook visual. Simplify until a creator could capture it in a single take on their phone.
- **The product doesn't need to be in frame.** Only include it when the action is naturally about it — and never held up to the camera or posed next to a screen. That's ad-brain.
- **Show the state, don't stage it.** The feeling should be visible in what she's doing, not performed for the lens. Ordinary, slightly unflattering, mid-life-moment beats set-dressed every time.

## How the three interlock

Each channel says something the others don't. Hide any one of them and the meaning should degrade — that's how you know each is earning its place.

The mechanics:

- The **overlay** buys the **verbal** its listen. Its open loop is why they pay attention to what's said.
- The **verbal** stays human because the **overlay** carries the exposition. The mouth never has to name the product.
- The **visual** proves the person and the moment both are pointing at.

Three interlock patterns, described by their moving parts:

**Pattern 1 — Name the unnamed.** The verbal is a real, vague, human sentence that points at something without naming it. The overlay supplies the missing noun. Together: instant clarity, zero ad-smell.

**Pattern 2 — Call out, then confess.** The overlay is a question or address that self-selects the viewer. The verbal pays it off with a vulnerable or hyper-relatable thought that only makes full sense because of the overlay.

**Pattern 3 — Claim, then react.** The overlay makes a first-person identity or feeling claim. The verbal reacts to it in the customer's own dialect. The visual cashes the claim on screen.

Patterns blend. The constants don't: division of labor, combined clarity, nothing that sounds like an ad.

## Where the words come from: voice of customer

Never write from imagination when real language exists. The best hooks steal their words — customers coin terms and phrases no copywriter would invent, and those exact phrases are what make the right person feel seen. In the best systems, the customer basically wrote the hook.

**Hunt before you write.** Check the project files and the conversation for, in order:

1. **A completed review audit / VOC audit** — insight themes plus verbatim customer quotes. This is the ideal input. If a persona gap analysis exists too, it tells you who to aim at.
2. **Raw sources** — customer reviews, ad comments, post-purchase surveys, or recorded sales and customer-success conversations as transcripts.

Then:

- **If an audit exists**, pull from it: the unique terms and standout phrases, and the insights they sit inside — pain points, trigger moments, objections, desired transformations.
- **If there are raw sources but no audit**, mine them right here: read for phrases you couldn't have invented, and note which pain point, trigger moment, objection, or transformation each one belongs to. Pull language verbatim — don't paraphrase the good stuff.
- **If there's truly nothing, stop and ask.** Request reviews, ad comments, survey exports, or call transcripts — even a handful helps. **Never fabricate customer language.** Invented slang reads fake and filters for nobody.

Mine two things from every source:

- **Language** — the exact words and coined terms. Work the stolen phrase into the verbal or the overlay — both when you can.
- **Cadence** — how these people talk. Sentence starts, rhythm, fillers, dialect. This is what the verbal is written in.

**The moment comes from the VOC too.** Customers describe when the pain surfaced and what finally triggered them — those scenes are where the visual and the verbal live. Don't invent a moment when the reviews hand you one.

**Persona is required.** Hunt for who this must speak to — a micro-persona from strategy docs, a gap analysis, or the conversation. If it's undefined, ask "who exactly is this for?" before writing. One sentence is enough: person + moment + feeling.

**Where ideas come from:** every hook system is built on one insight — a pain point, an objection, a desired transformation, or a trigger moment. The insight gives the idea. The VOC gives the words and the voice.

## The process

1. **Gather.** Persona, VOC, product basics. Hunt → mine → ask.
2. **Study the voice.** Before drafting anything, absorb how these customers talk — their words AND their cadence.
3. **Pick one insight** per hook system.
4. **Find the moment.** Where and when does this insight surface in their life, according to the VOC? That moment becomes the visual.
5. **Write the verbal.** The sentence that person would say out loud, in that moment, in their words and their rhythm.
6. **Write the overlay.** Pick its job — name / call out / claim — whichever completes the system. Ten words or fewer.
7. **Suggest the visual.** Framing + location + person + one action, capturable in a single take.
8. **Run the QA tests below.** Fix what fails, then output.

Sometimes the overlay comes first — when a lifted customer phrase is strong enough to *be* the overlay, build the verbal and visual around it.

---

## QA: every system passes all nine

1. **Speed test** — overlay is 10 words or fewer and parses at a glance.
2. **Overlap test** — the overlay adds something the verbal doesn't say: a noun, a target, or a claim. Nothing is duplicated between them.
3. **Out-loud test** — say the verbal. Would a person start talking like this on their phone? Stumble or stiffness = rewrite.
4. **Slap-on test** — would the person in the video plausibly type this overlay over her own post? Headline energy = rewrite.
5. **Cadence test** — the verbal's rhythm and opening trace to how these customers actually talk, not to a familiar hook formula. If it sounds like a hook you've heard before, rewrite it from the VOC.
6. **Set test** — across the full set, openings and cadences vary. No two systems start the same way.
7. **Filter test** — name who stops AND who scrolls. Both answers should come easily.
8. **One-take test** — the visual is one shot, one action, camera placement explainable in one phrase. Any "then... then..." = storyboard, rewrite.
9. **Receipt test** — the system traces back to a real VOC phrase or insight. Cite it.

## What an ad sounds like (never do these)

- **Attention without the filter.** Shock, loud noises, unrelated stunts. The wrong people stop and the right person doesn't feel seen.
- **Headline energy in the overlay.** Title case, numbered-guide framing, how-to phrasing, emojis, punctuation gymnastics — anything that reads like a title instead of context.
- **The overlay and verbal saying the same thing twice.** One of them is wasted — rewrite one.
- **A verbal that opens like ad copy.** Brand name first, a product introduction, or a question that diagnoses the viewer's problem at them.
- **Fake VOC.** Inventing "customer language" no customer ever said.
- **A staged visual.** Studio lighting, product held to camera, a scene built to display the product instead of the person's real moment.

---

## Output format

Default to 5 hook systems per run unless another number is asked for, spread across different insights and interlock patterns. Even when someone asks for "just a few hooks," deliver overlay + verbal as a pair — a single-channel hook is half a hook.

```
HOOK SYSTEM #1 — [the insight it's built on]

TEXT OVERLAY: "[10 words or fewer]"
VERBAL: "[the first sentence spoken]"
VISUAL: [framing + location + person + one action, 1–2 sentences]

FILTER: [who stops — person + moment + feeling]
VOC RECEIPT: "[the customer phrase or insight this came from]"
WHY IT WORKS: [one line — the interlock, the mechanism]
```

This file stays example-free — that's rule one, and it's what keeps every output sounding like the brand's customers instead of like the skill.
