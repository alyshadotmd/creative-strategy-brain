# Short-form scriptwriting

This skill covers how a short-form script works. It is voice-neutral, so it works for anyone.

- When the person has a voice guide (their own, a brand's or a creator's), read it too. That file makes it sound like them. This file makes it work as a short video.
- Every word also follows `voice-copy-standards`.
- Talk in plain everyday words. When the script comes in pieces, review one part at a time.

## The rules

1. **Write full spoken sentences.** Every line should sound like a real person talking out loud. "The video you're watching was edited with AI" is the right shape. Ad-copy style setups like a short teaser followed by a punchline fragment ("The video you're watching? Edited with AI.") read as written, not spoken. Lists of fragments ("No tool. No course. No experience.") become one natural sentence ("You don't need a tool, a course, or any experience").

2. **Questions are transitions.** A question moves the viewer into the next section, and the answer that follows is in full sentences. Example: "So what does that look like?" followed by a full explanation. A question is a turn in the conversation, never a teaser for a one-word answer.

3. **Every word earns its spot.** Short-form is tight. Cut the extra couple of words that make a line longer without adding meaning. Example: "This entire YouTube video was edited with AI" beats "This entire YouTube video that I just posted was edited with AI." Read each line and ask what it loses if the extra words go. If it loses nothing, cut them.

4. **Let the visual carry the context.** When something can be shown on screen (other people's videos popping up, a green screen behind the speaker, a text pop-up, a screen recording), the script states the point once and the visual fills in the detail. Example: "it feels like everyone on the internet realized they could edit videos with AI at the exact same time" stands alone while other creators' videos pop up on screen. A spoken description of what's already on screen gets cut.
   - Mark visual cues in the script in square brackets on their own line, like `[other creators' "edited with AI" videos pop up on screen]`, so the editor knows the visual is doing that job.
   - Long lists work best said partly out loud (3 or 4 items) with the full list shown as a text pop-up.

5. **Everything is sayable.** Parentheses, slashes, and asides that only work in writing become their own spoken sentence.

6. **Keep the speaker's real point.** Tighten wording, never swap the idea. When a line looks like a voice-to-text slip, keep the meaning, fix the words, and say what changed.

7. **Every line, CTAs included, sounds like the speaker and matches how the video will actually run.** Use words the speaker would really say out loud, and point people to where they'll really find the product (a CTA says "linked right here" only when there's a link). Example: "It's called [product], if you want to try it" fits a creator holding up the product; a scripted label like "If you're a 3am waker too" reads as fake.

8. **Show how a product works through the action itself.** The speaker discovers and uses it on camera, and a short natural line rides along with the action. Example: "Oh, it's a drink mix. Let me grab some water," then pour and stir on camera. That replaces a separate explainer line.

9. **A hook borrowed from a reference ad keeps the situation that makes it believable.** Before rewording a reference hook, name the story behind it, and carry that story into the new script. Example: a hydration brand's "I have the worst headache, so thank goodness this package just got here" works because the creator already uses the product, ran out, and the headache is what happened in the gap. A sleep supplement version keeps that story: "I've had the worst couple of nights of sleep, so thank goodness this package just got here," because the creator ran out of it. The speaker's own wording of the situation stays unless it breaks that story.

10. **A breakdown script teaches the move, the reason it works, and how to use it.** When a video explains why an ad works, each point names what the ad does, then says why that makes viewers believe or buy, so someone watching could recreate it. Example: "The ad pairs a feature with its benefit. A feature alone doesn't matter, because you don't know why to care. A benefit alone sounds fluffy. Show the feature that creates the benefit, and the benefit becomes believable." When the speaker's notes explain the strategy behind a moment, that reasoning stays in the script in the speaker's own logic (who the viewer is, what's holding them back, how the moment moves them), never swapped for a short summary line. When the notes include the wider lesson (for example, "pay attention to the questions in your comments, customer service chats and reviews, and turn them into ads"), that lesson stays too, because it's what lets viewers use the idea themselves. The runtime is fit by cutting whole beats, not by cutting the reasoning. When every beat matters and the full reasoning still runs past the target length, keep the reasoning, give the real runtime, and offer to split it into two videos or let the speaker pick which beat to drop. Example: a founder ad breakdown with five points kept all five in the speaker's words and split into two videos of about a minute each, instead of trimming each point to a one-line summary.

11. **A breakdown script talks about the ad's shopper in the third person.** The speaker is explaining the ad to people who make ads, so the person seeing the ad is "someone," "people," or "the shopper," and "you" is saved for the marketer watching the video. Example: "So when someone reads this, they know how the ad works before they've read a single word, and their brain goes straight to the comparison." This keeps it clear who is meant: the viewer is learning the move, not being sold to.

12. **A pattern breakdown keeps the spoken lines neutral and lets the visual name the example.** When a video teaches what a group of ads have in common, each line states the pattern in general terms ("they say exactly what the ingredients are"), and the example ad appears on screen in a visual cue. Brand names stay out of the spoken line. Example: "Instead of saying they have more ingredients, they say exactly what those ingredients are" with `[example ad on screen, ingredients row highlighted]`. A video that breaks down one specific brand's ad names that brand as usual.

13. **A line lands on the plain result, said the way a person would say it.** The end of a point states what changes for the viewer in ordinary words ("so anyone watching knows exactly which one to get"). A phrase built to sound clever or quotable, like wordplay on the product ("pick the size that fits how they live"), sounds like a slogan and reads as fake.

14. **The speaker's own wording is the script.** When the speaker describes what they want to say in their own words, those sentences become the lines, with only the tweaks needed to make them sayable and tight. Example: the speaker's note "Immediately people wonder, 'Why would a dog supplement company tell me not to give my dog a supplement?' and that makes them want to hear what the founder has to say" goes into the script almost as is, instead of a new line written from scratch.

15. **A breakdown point opens by naming the move.** Each point starts with what the move is called or what the ad does ("This is a contradictory hook"), then the reason it works. A rhetorical question in the speaker's voice is not how a point opens.

16. **Every line says exactly what happens, in literal words.** Figures of speech that leave the viewer to fill in the meaning ("that line is doing a lot of work") get replaced with what actually happens ("it makes the whole brand feel more authentic"). When a line introduces a source like a study, the next line connects to it out loud ("During this study, they found that..."), so the viewer never has to guess how two sentences relate.

17. **A summary keeps the full idea.** A TL;DR or recap repeats the real reasoning in short form, not a thinner label. Example: "the founder has something to lose" became "people want to see the founder put themselves on the line and back their product in public, and talk comfortably about the problem it solves, why they made it and how it's helped their customers." If a recap point feels shallow, the matching point earlier in the script usually needs the same depth.

18. **Name the exact subject.** Pick the word that is literally true: "the one person in the company who can't disappear" (not "in the ad"), "why they started the brand" (not "why they started"). When a quote stands for many examples, mark it as a type of line ("when a founder says something that sounds like 'I made this for you'") so it doesn't claim every founder says those exact words.

19. **Parallel points share one sentence shape.** When two lines do the same job, like two studies back to back, they use the same structure so the viewer hears them as a pair: "A study from 2019 found that..." then "A study from 2011, called the underdog effect, discovered that..." A credential that breaks the pattern (like "by Harvard researchers") comes out unless it's the point.

## How to respond

1. Give the revised section first, in a quote block.
2. Then list the changes as numbered things, each with the reason in one plain sentence.
3. Ask for the next part, or when the script is complete, give an estimated runtime and say where it could be shortened.

## When someone gives feedback on a script

Treat it as a lesson for this skill. Pull out the general principle, state it as what should be true, and add it to "The rules" above with a short example from their script.
